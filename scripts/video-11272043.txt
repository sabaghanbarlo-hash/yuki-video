Have you ever looked at an old photo of yourself and thought, "Who the hell is that?"

Not because the photo is bad. Okay, maybe it is. But because you genuinely don't feel like that person anymore.

You look at five-year-old you, who was crying because someone took their favorite toy, and think, "You were really going through all that?"

And the weird part is, technically, that's still you.

You have the same name. Same family. Same birthday. But your personality, interests, beliefs, appearance, and probably your entire taste in music have changed dramatically.

So at what point do you stop being the person you used to be?

Because if you think about it, we change constantly. Babies start out tiny, adorable, and completely useless at paying taxes. Then, over the years, we grow, our bodies change, our faces change, our personalities change, and eventually we're old people complaining about how expensive everything is.

Even our bodies are constantly replacing cells. We go through experiences that completely change how we think, and then years later, we forget most of them.

The five-year-old version of you probably had very strong opinions about cartoons, snacks, and why bedtime should be considered a human rights violation.

The twenty-five-year-old version of you might not even remember half of those opinions.

And the sixty-year-old version? They might look back at their twenty-five-year-old self and wonder what on earth they were doing.

Yet somehow, we treat ourselves as one continuous person.

We say, "I used to do this," "I remember that," and "I've changed."

But what exactly is the thing that connects all these different versions of us?

What makes you *you*?

One obvious answer is your body.

You look the same, at least generally speaking, and your body follows you throughout your life.

But let's test that idea.

If you lost your hair, would you still be you?

Obviously.

What if you lost a finger?

Still you.

A leg?

Still you.

At this point, you could be missing several body parts and still confidently say, "Yep, that's me."

So clearly, not every part of our body is equally important to our identity.

Now imagine a demon appears and says, "Congratulations. You can keep exactly one part of your body. Everything else is gone."

Which part would you choose?

I'm guessing most people aren't going to say, "Please preserve my left elbow. That's where the magic happens."

We'd probably choose our brain.

Because somewhere deep down, we believe that the brain is where the real us lives.

It's where we think, feel, remember, make decisions, and occasionally spend three hours replaying a conversation from 2019 that nobody else remembers.

We tend to think that if our brain survives, we might still be ourselves, even if everything else changes.

But even the brain isn't that simple.

What if you lost a skill?

Let's say you used to be an incredible pianist, but after an accident, you could no longer play.

Would you still be you?

Of course.

What if you forgot how to speak a language you once knew? Or how to cook your favorite meal?

Still you.

So maybe our identity isn't really about what we can do.

Maybe it's about what we remember.

Memories are a huge part of how we understand ourselves. We remember childhood, friendships, embarrassing moments, heartbreaks, our first love, and all those little experiences that somehow become part of our personal story.

But what if you lost all of them?

What if you woke up tomorrow and couldn't remember your childhood, your friends, your family, or anything that had happened to you?

Would you still be the same person?

This is where things get interesting.

Because even if you lost every memory, you might still have your personality.

You might still laugh at the same kinds of jokes. Get annoyed by the same things. Prefer quiet evenings over loud parties. Feel drawn to certain people. Care about kindness. Hate injustice. Have the same strange habits that your friends have been complaining about for years.

You wouldn't remember why you became that person.

But you might still be that person.

And this gives us a surprisingly interesting idea about personal identity.

Maybe who we are isn't defined by our appearance, our abilities, or even our memories.

Maybe it's defined by our character.

Our values. Our temperament. The things we care about. The way we respond to the world.

There's a famous philosophical idea associated with John Locke, who argued that personal identity has a lot to do with consciousness and the continuity of our mental life.

In simple terms, what makes you the same person isn't just having the same body. It's having a connection to your experiences, thoughts, and inner life.

But let's take this a step further.

Imagine you were given two choices.

Option one: You remember absolutely everything about your life, but your personality completely changes. You no longer care about the people you loved. Your values are different. Your humor is different. Everything that once mattered to you suddenly feels meaningless.

Option two: You forget every memory you've ever had, but your personality remains exactly the same. You still care about the same things, love the same kinds of people, and value the same principles.

Which one would you choose?

It's a difficult question.

Because memories are important. They're how we connect our past to our present. But our character is what determines how we experience the future.

And maybe that's why we care so much about being understood.

When someone says they love us, we don't want them to love our face, our body, our job, or the things we can do for them.

We want them to love something deeper.

The way we think. The way we feel. The little things that make us who we are.

We want someone to recognize our character and say, "I love *you*\."

Not just the version of you that exists today, but the person underneath all the temporary changes.

And this idea becomes especially interesting when we think about death.

We usually think of death as the ultimate loss of identity. Your body disappears. Your memories disappear. Your experiences come to an end.

And that's undeniably sad.

But what if the things that made you who you are were never entirely yours to begin with?

Think about the people who've influenced you.

Maybe you have your mother's sense of humor. Your father's stubbornness. A friend's way of looking at the world. A teacher who taught you to think differently. A book that completely changed your perspective.

You carry pieces of other people without even realizing it.

And those pieces influence the way you treat others, the decisions you make, and the things you believe.

Then, one day, you pass some of those things on.

Maybe you teach someone a lesson you once learned. Maybe you make someone laugh using a joke you inherited from a friend. Maybe you help someone because you remember how another person once helped you.

And that person carries it forward, too.

So in a strange way, our personalities, values, ideas, and ways of seeing the world don't necessarily disappear when we do.

They spread.

They live in other people.

Maybe the way you loved someone changes the way they love somebody else. Maybe something you said years ago becomes the advice someone gives their child. Maybe your kindness becomes a habit someone else carries throughout their life.

You may never know how far those little pieces of you travel.

And perhaps that's a comforting way to think about identity.

We're so attached to the idea that we're one permanent, unchanging person. We spend so much time trying to preserve who we are, worrying about how we're changing, and fearing that one day everything we know about ourselves will disappear.

But maybe change isn't something that threatens our identity.

Maybe change is what our identity is made of.

You don't have to remain the person you were at five, fifteen, or twenty-five.

You can outgrow your beliefs. Change your ambitions. Lose old interests. Discover new ones. Become softer after years of being defensive. Become stronger after years of being afraid.

And none of that means you've lost yourself.

Maybe you're simply becoming another version of yourself.

And perhaps the most important parts of you aren't the things that can be photographed, measured, or remembered.

They're the things you leave behind in other people.

The kindness you taught. The love you gave. The ideas you shared. The way you made someone feel when they thought nobody cared.

Your body will change. Your memories will fade. Your life will eventually come to an end.

But the things that made you who you are may continue to exist in ways you never expected.

Maybe we were never meant to be permanent.

Maybe we're just temporary homes for things that have been around long before us, and will continue long after us.

And honestly...

That's kind of beautiful.
