Okay, so today we're talking about how to get better at talking to people, which is a skill that apparently everyone is born knowing except me, because every time someone says "Hey, how are you?" my brain immediately opens 47 tabs and crashes.

Welcome to the human interaction tutorial. Unfortunately, there is no skip button.

First of all, I need you to understand something. You are not socially broken. You're not a defective Sims character who forgot to install the charisma expansion pack. You're a human being, and humans are actually pretty good at socializing. We just need practice.

Think about how babies learn to speak. Nobody sits a baby down with a PowerPoint presentation titled "English Grammar: The Horrors of Sentence Structure." Nobody gives them a vocabulary test and threatens to revoke their baby privileges if they fail.

They just spend time around people. They hear conversations. They watch people interact. And eventually, their brain starts connecting the dots. They learn that when someone says "How are you?" you don't actually have to give them a 40-minute documentary about your emotional state. You just say, "Good, how are you?" and everybody moves on with their lives.

Your brain is basically a giant pattern-detecting machine. It observes things, collects data, and slowly figures out how the world works. Socializing is no different. There are patterns in conversations. There are little unspoken rules. There are things that make people laugh, things that make them uncomfortable, and things that make them slowly turn around and walk away from you at a party.

The problem is that most of us haven't given our brains enough opportunities to learn those patterns.

And honestly, it's not entirely our fault.

Because look at how we communicate nowadays. We spend hours texting people, sending memes, liking posts, replying with "LMAO" when we're actually just sitting there with a neutral expression like a Victorian child who just saw a ghost.

Online communication is convenient. You can take five minutes to craft a response, delete it 17 times, ask ChatGPT to make it sound less desperate, and then send "yeah lol."

But real-life conversations don't work like that.

You can't pause someone mid-sentence and say, "Wait, let me rewrite my response. This version sounds too much like a hostage situation."

In real life, you're dealing with facial expressions, eye contact, body language, tone of voice, timing, personal space, and approximately 900 other things your brain has to process simultaneously.

Someone could say, "That's interesting," and depending on their tone, they could mean, "Wow, that's genuinely fascinating," or "I would rather be hit by a bus than continue this conversation."

And you have to figure out which one it is.

Texting doesn't teach you that. Texting teaches you how to interpret a skull emoji and whether three dots means someone's typing or they've been kidnapped.

So if you're great at texting but absolutely terrible at talking to people in person, congratulations\! You're bilingual. You're fluent in Internet, but your Real Life language skills are somewhere around Duolingo lesson three.

You can order a pizza online, but if the delivery guy asks you how your day is going, suddenly you're fighting for your life.

And the solution is surprisingly simple: practice.

I know. I know. You were hoping I'd reveal some secret technique. Maybe a magic phrase. Maybe there's a special handshake that makes everyone instantly love you. Maybe you could download a social skills update overnight.

But no. You have to actually talk to people.

A while ago, I challenged myself to interact with 100 people in one month. One hundred. Not make 100 friends. Not invite 100 people to my birthday party. Just have 100 interactions.

The bar was so low it was underground.

You could literally walk up to someone, say, "Hey, do you know where the library is?" They answer, you say thank you, and that's it. Congratulations. One interaction. You have gained one social XP. Your charisma stat has increased by 0.2%.

And yes, you can start with ridiculously simple interactions. Ask someone for directions. Compliment their shoes. Ask a cashier how their day is going. Talk to someone sitting next to you in class. You don't need to immediately approach a stranger and say, "Hello, I have prepared a 12-slide presentation explaining why we should become best friends."

Please don't do that. That's how you get a restraining order and a TED Talk nobody asked for.

The goal is simply to expose your brain to more real-life conversations.

At first, it'll feel awkward. You'll say something weird. They'll say something weird. You'll both pretend it didn't happen. And then you'll spend the next six years remembering it at 3 AM.

But eventually, something changes.

You start realizing that not every conversation needs to be perfect. You stop treating every interaction like a job interview for the position of "Most Likeable Human Being." You start listening instead of desperately searching your brain for the next sentence.

And sometimes, a conversation that starts with something incredibly basic can turn into something genuinely interesting.

You ask someone for directions, and they start telling you about their favorite restaurant nearby. You compliment someone's jacket, and suddenly you're talking about fashion. You ask a classmate about an assignment, and somehow you're both complaining about the education system for the next 45 minutes.

That's the beauty of interacting with people. You never know where a conversation might go.

Now, I'm not saying you should become the person who talks to literally everyone. You don't have to walk into a grocery store and interview every single person buying bananas. You don't have to become some extroverted social butterfly who wakes up at 6 AM excited to network.

You can still be introverted. You can still enjoy spending time alone. You can still go home after socializing and sit in silence for three hours like a rechargeable battery that's been completely drained.

The point isn't to change your personality. The point is to become comfortable enough that talking to people doesn't feel like a boss fight every time.

And here's something important: watching videos about social skills is helpful, but it can only take you so far.

Watching me explain how to talk to people is basically like watching someone ride a bicycle while you're sitting on the couch eating chips.

You might learn some useful things. You might understand the theory. You might even become an expert at identifying what other people are doing wrong.

But eventually, you're going to have to get on the bike.

And yes, you're probably going to fall. You're going to have awkward conversations. You're going to accidentally say "you too" when the waiter tells you to enjoy your meal. You're going to wave at someone who wasn't waving at you. You're going to tell a story that gets absolutely zero reaction and then wonder if the entire human race has collectively decided to hate you.

But that's normal. That's part of learning.

Nobody becomes good at something without being bad at it first. You don't learn to cook by watching Gordon Ramsay yell at people for 12 hours. You don't learn to play guitar by staring at a guitar. And you don't learn to socialize by watching videos about socializing while avoiding every human being in your immediate vicinity.

At some point, you have to actually do the thing.

So here's your challenge. Over the next month, try to have 100 small interactions with people. Don't overthink it. Don't make every conversation a mission to find your soulmate, future business partner, or the person who will finally validate your existence.

Just talk.

Say hello. Ask questions. Make small talk. Practice listening. Be curious about people. And when you have an awkward interaction, don't treat it like a permanent stain on your social record.

Nobody else is keeping score. Most people are too busy worrying about their own awkward moments to remember yours.

Your brain needs experience. It needs repetition. It needs to see enough conversations to eventually go, "Oh, I think I understand how this works."

And the more you practice, the less terrifying it becomes.

Eventually, talking to people won't feel like you're trying to speak a foreign language while being chased by a bear. It'll just feel like talking.

And that is the secret.

Not some mysterious personality trait. Not being born charming. Not having perfect jokes or an impressive wardrobe or the ability to make eye contact without looking like you're trying to challenge someone to a duel.

Just practice.

So go out there. Talk to people. Be awkward. Survive. Repeat.

And if you accidentally make a friend along the way, that's great.

If not, at least you got your social XP.

And if you did the challenge and still have no friends, well, at least now you're better at asking for directions.
