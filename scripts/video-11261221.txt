There's something really strange happening in the job market right now.

Companies are complaining that nobody wants to work. Meanwhile, young people are sending out hundreds of applications and getting rejected by jobs that somehow require three years of experience for an entry-level position.

So you have employers saying, "We can't find good workers."

And Gen Z saying, "We literally cannot find work."

And both sides are staring at each other like two divorced parents arguing over who ruined the economy.

But what exactly is going on?

For a lot of young people, getting a job has become an incredibly frustrating experience. You spend years studying, graduate, update your resume, and finally decide to enter the workforce.

And then you see a job listing.

Entry-level position.

Requirements: bachelor's degree, five years of experience, proficiency in twelve different programs, leadership skills, excellent communication, problem-solving abilities, and the ability to work independently.

Salary: barely enough to afford groceries.

And you're sitting there thinking, "If this is entry-level, what exactly is the entry point? Do I need to be born inside the company?"

The problem is that employers want experienced workers, but many don't want to invest in training inexperienced ones.

They want someone who can immediately do the job, make no mistakes, ask no unnecessary questions, and somehow already know how the company's entire system works.

But where exactly are people supposed to gain that experience?

You can't magically become a professional without ever being allowed to work professionally.

It's like refusing to teach someone how to drive, then getting angry when they don't know how to operate a car.

And this isn't just about laziness or entitlement. A lot of Gen Z entered adulthood during a pretty unusual period.

The pandemic disrupted education, internships, social interaction, and early career opportunities. Some people spent important years of their lives studying through a laptop screen, barely interacting with anyone outside their family.

Then suddenly, they're expected to enter an office, communicate confidently, understand workplace expectations, network, interview, and behave like they've been working for twenty years.

It's a pretty dramatic transition.

Imagine spending your entire college experience attending lectures in pajamas, submitting assignments online, and communicating through group chats.

Then your first day at work arrives, and someone asks you to call a client.

And suddenly, your entire brain goes:

"Sorry, we don't have that feature installed."

This is where the generational conflict starts.

Older managers sometimes complain that young employees lack communication skills, initiative, professionalism, or the ability to handle criticism.

And to be fair, some of those complaints aren't completely unreasonable.

Some young workers struggle with communication. Some avoid uncomfortable conversations. Some expect immediate results, get frustrated easily, or don't understand that being employed means occasionally doing things you don't particularly enjoy.

Unfortunately, your job is not always going to be your passion.

Sometimes you're going to have to attend a meeting that could have been an email. Sometimes your boss will ask you to redo something you spent three hours working on. And sometimes you're going to have to pretend you're interested in a spreadsheet that looks like it was designed by Satan.

That's part of working.

But there's another side to this conversation that employers often overlook.

You can't expect someone to develop professional skills while providing them absolutely no guidance.

If a young employee makes a mistake, explain what went wrong. If they're struggling, give them feedback. If you expect them to take initiative, actually give them opportunities to do so.

Because saying, "You should know this already," isn't exactly a training program.

And this brings us to one of the most fascinating things about Gen Z in the workplace.

The Gen Z stare.

You've probably seen it.

You ask someone a question, and instead of responding, they just look at you.

No expression. No words. No movement.

Just staring.

It's like their soul has temporarily disconnected from the Wi-Fi.

And naturally, the internet turned this into another generational debate.

Some people interpret it as disrespect, laziness, or a lack of social skills.

But sometimes, that expression is simply someone processing information. Someone who's nervous, confused, overwhelmed, or unsure what response is expected of them.

Not every silence is an act of rebellion.

Sometimes the person isn't ignoring you. They're just trying to figure out what you said before their brain crashes.

And honestly, this is where we need to be careful about turning an entire generation into a stereotype.

Gen Z isn't one personality type.

There are incredibly hardworking young people. There are lazy young people. There are people who are confident, people who are anxious, people who are ambitious, and people who are just trying to get through the week without having a mental breakdown over an email.

Congratulations. They're humans.

But the job market itself is changing, and that's creating another problem.

Artificial intelligence.

Companies are increasingly using AI to automate tasks that were traditionally assigned to entry-level workers.

Writing basic reports. Organizing information. Answering simple questions. Performing repetitive administrative tasks.

And while AI can make businesses more efficient, it also raises an uncomfortable question.

If entry-level jobs are where people traditionally gained experience, what happens when those jobs start disappearing?

You can't demand that someone have five years of experience while simultaneously removing the opportunities that allow them to gain five years of experience.

It's like removing the tutorial from a video game and then wondering why nobody knows how to defeat the final boss.

The job market is becoming increasingly competitive, and young people are expected to have more skills than ever before.

A degree isn't always enough. A resume isn't always enough. Even having experience doesn't guarantee that you'll get hired.

And the worst part is that many people are doing everything they're told to do.

They study. They graduate. They apply. They improve their resumes. They learn new skills.

And then they get an automated rejection email that arrives faster than their food delivery.

"Unfortunately, we have decided to move forward with other candidates."

Other candidates?

WHO ARE THESE OTHER CANDIDATES?

Are they living in the walls? Are they secretly trained professionals who were born with Excel installed in their brains?

Because at this point, getting rejected from a job you weren't even qualified for is starting to feel like a personal attack.

But despite all of this, I don't think the solution is to blame either generation.

Gen Z needs to understand that workplaces have expectations. Communication matters. Reliability matters. Being able to accept criticism matters. And not every inconvenience is a sign that your employer is toxic.

But employers also need to understand that young workers are entering a very different world from the one they entered.

Housing is expensive. Competition is intense. Technology is changing entire industries. And the traditional promise of "get a degree, get a job, buy a house, and live happily ever after" doesn't exactly feel realistic anymore.

People are trying to build careers in an environment where the rules keep changing.

And maybe instead of constantly arguing about whether Gen Z is lazy or whether employers are unreasonable, we should actually address the problem.

Companies need to invest in training.

Young workers need opportunities to learn.

Managers need to communicate expectations clearly.

And everyone needs to stop expecting inexperienced people to somehow become experienced without ever being given a chance.

Because at the end of the day, the workplace doesn't need another generation of people pretending they know everything.

It needs people who are willing to learn, employers who are willing to teach, and maybe just a little less hostility toward anyone who doesn't know how to operate a printer.

Because let's be honest.

Nobody knows how to operate a printer.

Not Gen Z. Not Millennials. Not Boomers.

That machine is evil, and we should all agree on that.

Anyway, if you're a young person struggling to find a job right now, you're not necessarily doing something wrong.

The market is genuinely difficult.

Keep learning. Build skills outside of your degree. Get whatever experience you can, even if it's through small projects. Learn how to communicate professionally. And don't let a hundred rejection emails convince you that you're incapable of doing something.

You might not have five years of experience yet.

But you have to start somewhere.

And hopefully, one day, someone will finally give you the opportunity to do that.

Until then, good luck out there.

You're going to need it.
