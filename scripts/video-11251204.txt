Apparently, nobody wants to hire Gen Z.

We're lazy. We're entitled. We don't know how to communicate. We can't handle criticism, and apparently, looking at someone without smiling is now a workplace offense.

There's even a name for it: the Gen Z stare.

You know the one. Your manager asks you to do something, and instead of responding with an enthusiastic "Absolutely! I'd love to!" you just look at them.

And somehow, that's become evidence that an entire generation is unemployable.

But here's something interesting. Gen Z is actually one of the most educated generations in history. They're comfortable with technology, they're learning new digital skills, and many are working side hustles just to afford existing.

So how did we go from being the generation that grew up with technology to the generation that apparently can't operate a printer without emotional support?

Maybe the problem isn't as simple as everyone thinks.

Let's imagine a young woman named Sophie.

She's 22, recently graduated from university, and just landed her first proper office job.

She's excited. She's nervous. She's bought a couple of professional-looking outfits and probably spent three hours researching what people are supposed to wear to an office.

On her first day, she arrives early, gets her laptop, and is shown where the bathroom is.

Then someone sends her a link to a company training video.

It's 40 minutes long.

And that's basically her training.

She has approximately 700 questions, but nobody seems particularly available to answer them.

Should she message her manager directly? Is it okay to leave at five? Does everyone eat lunch together? Is she supposed to reply to emails immediately? And why does everyone seem to know what's happening except her?

She doesn't want to look incompetent, so she spends the entire day pretending she understands everything.

Welcome to adulthood, Sophie. The tutorial has been skipped.

And this is where things start going wrong.

Because there's something people often forget when talking about young employees.

You don't magically develop professional skills the moment you receive your first paycheck.

You have to learn them somewhere.

How to communicate with coworkers, handle criticism, participate in meetings, manage deadlines, and deal with difficult situations.

These things aren't always taught in university.

They're usually learned through experience, observation, and having someone around who can actually show you what to do.

But Gen Z entered adulthood during a particularly strange period.

Millions of students experienced remote education during the pandemic. Their classrooms became computer screens, their internships became Zoom meetings, and their social interactions were suddenly reduced to tiny squares with muted microphones.

Some missed years of ordinary social and educational experiences.

And then, after graduating, they were expected to enter the professional world and behave like experienced employees.

Imagine spending several years learning how to communicate through a screen and then suddenly being expected to confidently present ideas in front of 15 strangers.

It's a pretty big adjustment.

And sometimes, when young employees struggle with it, people interpret their nervousness as laziness or disrespect.

Which brings us back to the Gen Z stare.

Have you ever been so nervous that your brain temporarily stops functioning?

Someone asks you a question, and suddenly you forget every language you've ever learned.

Your mouth is closed. Your eyes are open. Your brain is displaying a Windows error message.

That's a perfectly normal stress response.

Psychologists call one version of this reaction freezing.

And sometimes, what looks like an attitude problem is actually someone who's anxious, confused, or trying very hard not to say something embarrassing.

Of course, that doesn't mean every awkward interaction should be excused.

Professional communication is a skill, and young employees still have to develop it.

But perhaps we should stop treating every uncomfortable facial expression like evidence of a generational collapse.

Now, let's look at the other side of the situation.

Meet Mark.

He's 45, manages a department, and has 14 employees reporting to him.

He spends most of his day answering emails, attending meetings, fixing problems, and trying to finish his own work.

And now he's also responsible for training Sophie.

The problem?

Nobody has actually taught Mark how to train people.

He was promoted because he was good at his previous job. Suddenly, he's expected to become a manager, mentor, therapist, and professional problem solver.

And he has approximately 12 minutes of free time per week.

So when Sophie makes a mistake, he corrects her quickly and moves on.

When she asks a question, he tells her to check the documentation.

When she doesn't understand something, he assumes she should already know it.

From his perspective, Sophie isn't taking enough initiative.

From Sophie's perspective, nobody is explaining anything.

And both of them are getting increasingly frustrated.

This is where the problem becomes bigger than one inexperienced employee or one impatient manager.

Many workplaces have become increasingly demanding while offering less time for training and mentorship.

Companies want employees who can immediately contribute.

They want people who know how to use the software, communicate with clients, solve problems, and produce excellent work.

But they don't necessarily want to spend the time and money required to develop those employees.

Basically, they want the finished product without paying for the manufacturing process.

And this problem becomes even more interesting when we introduce artificial intelligence.

Think about the tasks traditionally given to junior employees.

Writing first drafts, organizing spreadsheets, researching information, preparing presentations, and checking basic reports.

These tasks might seem boring, but they're also how people learn.

You write something. Someone more experienced corrects it. You understand what you did wrong. You improve.

After doing this for months, you gradually become better at your job.

But now, AI can perform many of those basic tasks in seconds.

And while that can make experienced employees more productive, it also raises an important question.

If AI does all the beginner-level work, how exactly are beginners supposed to gain experience?

You can't expect someone to have five years of experience when you refuse to give them their first five months of practice.

And this isn't just a Gen Z problem.

It's a problem that could affect the entire future workforce.

Because today's inexperienced graduates are tomorrow's managers, engineers, designers, and business owners.

If nobody invests in developing them, eventually there won't be enough experienced people to replace the ones leaving.

But there's another side we should acknowledge.

Gen Z isn't automatically right about everything.

Some young employees really do struggle with professional communication.

Some expect promotions before they've demonstrated their abilities.

Some struggle with criticism, punctuality, or basic workplace responsibilities.

And sometimes, managers have legitimate reasons to be frustrated.

Being young doesn't mean you're exempt from accountability.

However, expectations should work both ways.

You can't expect employees to show initiative if they're punished every time they make a mistake.

You can't expect people to become confident if nobody gives them constructive feedback.

And you can't demand experience while refusing to provide opportunities to gain it.

There's also an interesting generational misunderstanding happening here.

Older generations often grew up with a different relationship to work.

For many, staying late, accepting difficult conditions, and prioritizing work over personal time were considered normal parts of building a career.

Gen Z tends to question some of those expectations.

They want reasonable working hours, clear communication, opportunities to grow, and enough money to actually enjoy the life they're working for.

And honestly, can you blame someone for questioning a job that pays just enough to cover rent and groceries?

Of course, wanting a healthy work-life balance doesn't mean you can ignore your responsibilities.

But neither does being a good employee mean sacrificing your entire existence to make a company happy.

And here's something worth remembering: every generation has been criticized by the one before it.

Millennials were called entitled.

Generation X was called lazy and directionless.

Young baby boomers were criticized for being rebellious and self-absorbed.

And now it's Gen Z's turn.

Apparently, every generation reaches adulthood, forgets what being young felt like, and immediately starts complaining about the next generation.

It's practically a historical tradition.

So, what can young people actually do to survive their first jobs?

First, ask questions.

I know it feels uncomfortable, especially when everyone around you looks busy, but asking for clarification is much better than spending three hours doing something incorrectly.

Second, ask your manager what success actually looks like.

What should you be able to accomplish in your first 30, 60, and 90 days?

Get clear expectations instead of trying to guess what everyone wants.

Third, find someone experienced who's willing to help you.

A good mentor can teach you things that no university course or YouTube tutorial ever will.

And finally, learn to communicate your progress.

If you've completed something, say so. If you're stuck, explain why. If you need help, ask before a small problem becomes a massive one.

These are simple habits, but they can make a huge difference.

And if you're a manager watching this, remember something too.

Your new employee isn't supposed to arrive knowing everything.

They are new. That's literally why they're called new employees.

Give them instructions. Explain mistakes. Provide feedback. Let them learn.

You might be surprised by how capable someone becomes when they're actually given the opportunity to develop.

Because the real problem isn't that Gen Z doesn't want to work.

It's that we're entering a world where companies increasingly expect experienced workers but are becoming less willing to create them.

And blaming an entire generation is much easier than admitting that something about the system isn't working.

So maybe the next time a young employee gives you the Gen Z stare, don't immediately assume they're disrespecting you.

Maybe they're confused.

Maybe they're nervous.

Or maybe you just asked them to complete a spreadsheet without explaining where the spreadsheet is.

We really don't know.

But one thing is certain: every experienced employee was once an inexperienced one.

And somebody, somewhere, had to give them a chance.

So let's stop arguing about which generation is the worst and start asking a much more useful question:

What kind of workplace are we actually building for the next generation?

Because eventually, the young people everyone complains about today will be the ones running the place tomorrow.

And I, for one, would prefer not to have my future boss be someone who learned everything from a 40-minute training video.
